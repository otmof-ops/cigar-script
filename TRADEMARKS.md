# Trademark policy

**CigarScript** (the language, also written cigar-script, its repository's
name, and cigar, its command) and the CigarScript logo are trademarks of Jay
Taylor, as **CigScript** is. The code is Apache-2.0, and the Apache license
says so itself in its section 6: it grants no trademark rights. This page says
what that means in practice, so nobody has to guess.

The short version: use the names to say what your thing is made of or works
with; do not use them to say what your thing *is*.

## Free to do, no permission needed

- Say that a program **is written in CigarScript**, or that a tool **is
  compatible with CigarScript**, when that is accurate.
- Write programs, libraries, editor support, examples and teaching material
  in and for CigarScript. The syntax, the strength names and the error codes
  are not claimed as trademarks or as protected expression, and anyone may
  implement the language.
- Use the names in articles, talks, tutorials, comparisons, reviews and
  criticism, including unflattering ones.
- Distribute unmodified official releases under their names, with the NOTICE
  file intact, as Apache-2.0 requires.
- Name a package, plugin or repository in a way that describes its
  relationship truthfully, such as `cigarscript-vscode`, `cigar-fmt` or
  `awesome-cigarscript`, provided it is clear the project is not the official
  one.

## Ask first

- Naming a **fork, another implementation, a product, service, company or
  domain** "CigarScript", "cigar-script" or a confusingly similar name
  (CigarScripts, cigarlang, cigarscript.dev). Forks and other implementations
  are welcome under Apache-2.0; they need their own name once they differ from
  the official one.
- Using the **logo** anywhere other than to link to the official project.
- Calling a build, distribution or service **official**, **certified**, or
  otherwise implying endorsement.
- Merchandise carrying the names or the logo.

Use GitHub's private reporting on this repository (see `SECURITY.md`) or open
a discussion with what you have in mind. Answers are usually quick, and the
usual answer to a truthful, non-confusing use is yes.
